## Who's actually moving in

**House sparrows.** Small, brown, loud, and the most common bird in a residential call. They love a cavity, so dryer and bathroom vents, holes in soffits and the gaps behind decorative trim are all fair game. They nest from roughly April through August and will raise more than one brood in the same spot.

**European starlings.** A bit bigger, speckled and glossy, and pushier. They go for gable vents, open fascia ends and anything around the HVAC on a roof. In winter, flocks of them roost on the sides of heated commercial buildings, sometimes by the hundreds.

**Rock pigeons.** Around all year. Pigeons nest on ledges, balcony corners, sign brackets, parking garage beams and, more and more, under rooftop solar panels. Once a few settle in, more follow.

**Gulls.** Ring-billed and herring gulls nest on flat commercial roofs and hang around parking lots near food courts. They get loud and protective from about May to July.

**Canada geese.** Stormwater ponds, sports fields and office-park lawns. Territorial in nesting season and messy all the time.

**Chimney swifts.** We run into these now and then in older homes with uncapped chimneys. They're a protected species, so a swift nest in a chimney gets left alone until they're done for the season.

Why bother pinning down the species? Because the rules are different for each one, and so is the fix.

## Can you pull the nest out yourself? Depends on the bird

This is the question I get the most, and the honest answer is "maybe."

Most native birds, including gulls, geese and chimney swifts, are covered by the federal [Migratory Birds Convention Act](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/SOR-2022-105/section-63.html). You can't disturb one of their nests while there are eggs or young in it without a permit, and those permits take time. Ontario's Fish and Wildlife Conservation Act adds provincial protection on top for most wild birds, and the province has a plain-language page on [when you can remove a bird's nest or eggs](https://www.ontario.ca/page/remove-bird-nests-or-eggs) that's worth a read.

Pigeons, house sparrows and starlings are introduced species, and the federal act doesn't cover them. That's why we have more room to work with a sparrow nest in a vent than a robin nest on a light fixture. Even so, if there are eggs or chicks, we slow down and think about timing before anything comes out.

My rule of thumb:

- **Not sure what bird it is?** Leave it, take a photo and send it to us.
- **Eggs or babies?** Leave it until we've had a look.
- **Native bird nesting season** runs roughly April through July. Work before or after that is a lot simpler.

It's also worth knowing that Vaughan was named a Bird-Friendly City by Nature Canada in 2023, and the city has [a page on what that means](https://www.vaughan.ca/about-city-vaughan/departments/environmental-sustainability/bird-friendly-city) for residents. The city doesn't remove birds from private property, though. That falls to the owner. Vaughan does require wildlife removal companies to hold a city licence, so it's fair to ask anyone you hire to show you theirs.

## Gaps behind the stone and trim

Here's something that's pretty specific to newer Vaughan homes. A lot of houses built over the last twenty years have decorative precast or stone pieces on the front: window headers, keystones, bands of stone between the floors, sills under the windows. They look great. But the builder usually leaves a small gap behind or above them, and after a few winters the caulking dries out and that gap gets bigger.

To a sparrow, that's a perfect little shelf with a roof over it.

I had a call out to Kleinburg where the owner kept hearing chirping inside the wall of the front bedroom every morning and thought it might be mice. From the lawn you could just see a few strands of straw hanging down from the top edge of the stone header over that window. When I got up on the ladder, sparrows had stuffed the whole space behind the precast with nesting material, back into the wall cavity. ## Vents, dryer ducts and the fire question

Vent nests are the most common residential bird call we get, every spring. A vent hood on the side of a house is a dry, sheltered tube with a door that sparrows and starlings figure out quickly.

It's more than an annoyance. Nesting material plus dryer lint is a fire risk. A packed bathroom duct can't pull moisture out, so you get mould on the ceiling. And when the chicks hatch, they're loud and right there in the wall.

Signs a vent has a tenant:

- The dryer takes two runs to dry a load, or there's a hot, burnt smell
- Straw, feathers or twigs hanging from the vent hood or on the ground below it
- Chirping or fluttering behind a bathroom fan, mostly early in the morning
- Little bits of fluff blowing out when the fan or dryer kicks on

Our fix is to confirm the bird and whether there are young, clear the full length of the duct right back to the fan or dryer, clean and disinfect it, and fit a heavy metal vent guard that lets air out and keeps birds out. The thin plastic covers sold at the hardware store tend to crack in a hard winter or plug up with lint, so I'd pass on those.

## Pigeons, panels and balconies

Solar panels have become one of my regular pigeon calls. The gap between the panel and the shingles is shaded, dry, protected from wind, and pigeons can walk right in from the edge.

A homeowner in Vellore Village called because the sound of cooing seemed to be coming from inside the roof, and there were droppings sliding down the shingles below the panels every time it rained. When we got up there, a few pairs of pigeons were nesting under the array, with a thick mat of droppings and twigs underneath. Once the nests were empty, we cleared and cleaned under the panels and installed a mesh skirt clipped around the full edge of the array, so there's airflow but no way in. The condos around the Vaughan Metropolitan Centre have the more familiar version: pigeons nesting in balcony corners, behind planters and under stored furniture. What works there:

- **Netting** across the open side of the balcony. Fine black mesh, hard to see from the ground, and the only thing that keeps pigeons off completely.
- **Spikes or tensioned wire** on railings and ledges where netting isn't allowed.
- **Clearing clutter.** A stored table or a stack of boxes in a corner is basically an invitation.

Condo boards usually have rules about what can go on a balcony. We're used to working with property managers to get an install approved first.

## Big roofs in Concord

Concord and the industrial areas along the 400 and 407 have a lot of large, flat roofs, warehouses with open loading bays, and parking structures. That's a different job from a house.

- **Pigeons** roost in loading docks, under canopies and in the steel above warehouse racking. Droppings end up on inventory, equipment and trucks.
- **Gulls** nest right on the roof membrane or gravel, and roofs with ponding water draw them in even more. Once eggs are down, you're into permit territory.
- **Starlings** pack into gaps around rooftop units and signage.
- **Geese** take over lawns next to stormwater ponds and get aggressive with staff in the spring.

On these jobs the answer is usually a plan that runs over a season or two: netting under canopies and in bays, wire grids on parts of the roof gulls like, spikes on ledges and signs, and fixing drainage where water sits. For gulls and geese, the trick is starting in late winter, before nests go down. Geese also respond to changes in the layout, like taller plantings along a pond edge or low fencing between water and lawn, more than to any amount of chasing.

## Why the droppings matter

Bird droppings are acidic. Over time they eat into paint, metal railings, stone and concrete, and they stain stucco and brick. On a flat roof they clog drains, and in a Vaughan winter, backed-up water turns into ice and leaks.

There's a health side as well. Built-up droppings can carry the fungus behind histoplasmosis, plus bacteria like salmonella and E. coli. Fresh droppings on a deck aren't much of a risk. The trouble starts when a dry pile gets swept or blasted with a pressure washer and the dust goes airborne. Old nests also carry mites and lice, which don't always leave with the birds.

So for a big cleanup, we wear respirators and full gear, wet things down or HEPA-vacuum instead of sweeping, and finish with a disinfectant and an odour treatment. The smell is part of what calls birds back.

## How bird removal in Vaughan goes when we do it

1. **You call or send photos.** We'll ask what bird you think it is, where it's nesting, and whether it's getting inside.
2. **Inspection.** On a house that's a ladder and a walk around, plus the attic if birds are getting in. On a commercial building it can mean roof access and a camera on a pole. We figure out the species, whether the nest is active, and every gap or landing spot, including the ones you can't see from the ground.
3. **A written plan and quote,** with timing worked around nesting season if that matters.
4. **Getting them out.** For birds inside a soffit or attic, we can use a one-way exclusion device once we know there are no eggs or young that would be trapped. The birds leave on their own and can't get back in. If a nest is active and protected, we wait for the young to leave and put interim measures in place, like partial barriers and cleanup.
5. **Closing it up.** Metal vent guards, flashing over soffit gaps, mesh behind signs and under panels, netting over balconies and bays. Everything has to handle freeze and thaw, heavy snow and summer sun, so we use metal and commercial-grade netting.
6. **Making ledges useless.** Spikes, tensioned wire or low-profile electric track on the places birds like to sit.
7. **Cleanup and a walk-through.** We show you what we did and what to keep an eye on.

A simple vent nest is often one visit of one to three hours. An attic or roofline job is usually an inspection plus one or two visits. Big commercial sites can take several days and are sometimes phased so the business keeps running. Waiting on a nest to empty adds time.

## Gimmicks that don't last

Plastic owls, reflective tape, scare balloons and ultrasonic boxes. Pigeons and sparrows get used to all of them, usually within days. I've seen spikes and netting put up wrong too, with gaps or sagging sections the birds just walk around. And blocking a vent before the nest is out can trap birds inside, which is a much worse problem.

What you can do yourself:

- Clear an empty pigeon nest off your own balcony, with gloves and a mask
- Wash small amounts of fresh droppings with soap and water
- Move clutter that's creating a sheltered corner
- Stop feeding birds near the house

What I'd leave to us: anything with eggs or chicks where you're not sure of the species, anything past the first-storey ladder, big piles of dried droppings indoors, and hardware that has to last through a few winters.

## Quick answers

**Will the birds come back?**
They'll try, especially pigeons. If the spot is sealed or netted and cleaned, they give up and go somewhere else. When birds do come back, it's almost always a missed gap, the wrong deterrent for that bird, or a cleanup that left droppings behind.

**How fast can you come out?**
A blocked dryer vent or aggressive birds at a business entrance gets bumped up. Preventive work is usually booked within a few business days.

**When should I bird-proof?**
Late winter into early April, before nesting starts. That's when it's easiest and cheapest.

**Are spikes and electric track cruel?**
No. Spikes don't stab anything, they just leave a bird nowhere flat to stand. The track gives a mild jolt that's unpleasant enough to make a pigeon pick another ledge and doesn't injure it.

**What if a bird died in the vent or the wall?**
It happens, usually when someone blocked an opening with a bird still inside. You'll know by the smell within a few days. We open the vent or the smallest access point we can, remove the bird and the nest, and treat the area so the smell doesn't linger.

**Is there anything else in there?**
Sometimes. The same gaps birds use can let in squirrels and raccoons too, so we'll tell you if we see signs of other animals while we're up there.
