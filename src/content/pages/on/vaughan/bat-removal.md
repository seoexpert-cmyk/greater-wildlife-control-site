## What the droppings are telling you

Bat droppings and mouse droppings look a lot alike at first glance. Both are small, dark and roughly rice-shaped. The difference shows up when you press one with the tip of a screwdriver or a pencil. A mouse dropping is hard and holds together. A bat dropping falls apart into a dry powder full of tiny glittery bits, which are the leftover wings and shells of the insects it ate.

Where they land matters too. Mice leave droppings along baseboards and in cupboards. Bat droppings pile up under the spot where bats come and go, so you'll find them on a ledge, a deck board, the top of an air conditioner or a patch of mulch right below a gap in the roofline.

Here's a job that shows how that works. A homeowner in Woodbridge kept finding a small heap of droppings on the outside ledge of an upstairs window, and wiping it off every few days. She'd assumed birds were sitting on the gable above it. When we looked up, the screen on the gable vent had a torn bottom corner, and the wood around it was stained dark and shiny. That night we watched bats crawl out of that corner one after another and drop straight down past her window before they flew off. <!-- generic anecdote: confirm or replace with a real job -->

Other things that point to a colony instead of a passing bat:

- **Dark, greasy marks around a gap.** The oils in a bat's fur rub off every time it squeezes through, so a regular exit gets a stain over time.
- **Sounds at dusk and dawn.** A thin squeaking, or a papery rustle in a wall or the attic, mostly right around sunset and again just before sunrise.
- **A sharp smell.** Bat urine has an ammonia bite to it. If you can smell it from the upstairs hallway, they've probably been there more than one summer.
- **Repeat visitors inside.** One bat in the house might be a stray. Two or more in a season almost always means a colony in the structure.

## Two bats, two sets of rules

In York Region, two species turn up in houses far more than the rest.

The **big brown bat** is the one we handle most. It's a sturdy animal, it doesn't mind a chilly attic, and some of them spend the winter inside a building instead of heading to a cave. A colony in a house can be anything from a handful of bats to a few hundred.

The **little brown myotis** used to be everywhere. White-nose syndrome hit it hard, and it's now listed as endangered under [Ontario's Endangered Species Act](https://www.ontario.ca/laws/regulation/260060). The northern myotis and the tri-coloured bat are on that list as well. Other species like the eastern red, hoary and silver-haired bats live around here too, but they mostly roost in trees, not attics.

Every bat in Ontario is protected under the Fish and Wildlife Conservation Act. So killing them, poisoning them or trapping them isn't on the table, and with the endangered species the penalties are steeper. The only legal way to deal with a colony in a house is exclusion: give the bats a way out that doesn't let them back in, and seal everything else.

That's also why knowing which bat it is matters. If there's any chance it's a little brown, we follow the province's [bat exclusion guidelines](https://files.ontario.ca/environment-and-energy/species-at-risk/mnr_sar_tx_bat_exclu_enfr.pdf) to the letter, and we'd do the same for a big brown anyway.

## When bat removal in Vaughan can happen, and when it has to wait

Here's the part that frustrates people the most. You can't just evict bats whenever it suits you. Their year sets the schedule.

- **April to early May.** Bats are waking up and moving into summer roosts. If they're flying every night, this is an early window for exclusion, before any babies arrive.
- **Mid-May to late August.** Maternity season. Pregnant females cluster in warm attics and wall spaces, the pups are born in June, and they can't fly until somewhere around mid-August. Put a one-way door on the house now and the mothers go out to feed and can't get back to their young. The pups starve inside your walls. That's illegal, and you'd smell the result for weeks.
- **Late August through October.** The main window. The young are flying, everyone leaves at night, and the whole colony can be excluded together. In a mild fall we can sometimes keep going into November.
- **Winter.** Any bats still in the building are hibernating and won't come out through a door. Pushing them out into the cold kills them. Winter is for inspections, quotes and sealing the gaps nothing is using.

### So what if you find them in June?

You wait, but you don't sit on your hands. We can close off the routes from the attic into the living space: the attic hatch, gaps around pot lights, open spots around plumbing and wiring. That keeps bats in the roof and out of your bedrooms. Sometimes we also seal secondary gaps that clearly aren't being used. Then we come back once the pups are flying and do the full job.

## Woke up with a bat in the room?

This one jumps the queue, because rabies is the real concern here, not the bat itself.

Rabies in bats isn't common, but it's fatal once symptoms show up, and a bat bite can be so small you'd never notice it. So the question isn't whether the bat looks sick. It's whether anyone could have had contact with it.

1. **Shut the door** to the room and keep kids and pets out.
2. **Don't touch it bare-handed.** If you need to catch it, wear thick leather work gloves, wait for it to land, set a container over it and slide a piece of cardboard underneath.
3. **If someone was asleep in that room, if a small child or someone who can't tell you was in there, or if anyone touched it,** keep the bat and call [York Region Public Health](https://www.york.ca/health/infectious-diseases-and-prevention/rabies-control). They'll tell you whether it needs testing and whether anyone needs shots.
4. **If nobody was exposed,** turn the lights off, open the window wide and leave the room. Most bats find their own way out.
5. **Then call us.** A bat inside, especially one in winter, usually means there's a colony somewhere in the house and a path from there into your rooms.

## Newer house, same problem

People are often surprised when we find bats in a house that's only five or ten years old. "It's brand new, how are they getting in?" The answer is that a newer house has a lot more seams than an old one.

Think about a typical home in Maple or the newer parts of Kleinburg. Several gables, a couple of dormers, a mix of brick, stone veneer and siding, aluminum fascia and soffit, a ridge vent across the top. Every place two materials meet is a joint, and joints move. After a few winters of freeze and thaw, aluminum fascia starts to pull away from the board behind it, flashing lifts at a dormer, and a ridge vent's end cap works loose.

A bat doesn't chew its way in like a squirrel. It just needs a gap of about a centimetre. If you can slide a carpenter's pencil into a gap on edge, a bat can probably get through it.

I remember a house in Maple where the owners had a bat inside twice in one July and were sure it was coming down the chimney. There was no chimney. When I went up the ladder, a long run of aluminum fascia along the back of the house had bowed out from the wood behind it, leaving a slot you couldn't see from the yard. You could hear them chattering behind it, and the bottom edge had that greasy shine to it. Because it was July, we sealed the attic side and came back in late August to put the doors on. <!-- generic anecdote: confirm or replace with a real job -->

Older homes have their own weak spots. A lot of 1960s and 70s houses in Woodbridge have long, low rooflines, wood soffits that have warped, and mortar joints and chimney crowns that have seen fifty winters. Different house, same idea.

Places I check on almost every Vaughan job:

- Where the fascia, soffit and roof edge come together, especially at corners
- Gable vents with torn or wide screening
- Ridge vent ends and lifted shingles along the ridge
- Valley and dormer flashing
- Where stone veneer meets brick or siding
- Chimney crowns and caps
- Gaps around plumbing stacks, electrical masts and furnace vents

## From the ladder to the last seal

Here's how a bat job goes with us, start to finish.

1. **The first conversation.** What you've seen, where, and when. Whether anyone's had contact with a bat. Roughly how old the house is and what it's clad in.
2. **The inspection.** Ladders, the roof where it's safe to walk, and the attic. We're looking at droppings, staining and the condition of the insulation, and building a list of every possible entry point, used or not.
3. **A dusk watch.** We stand outside at sunset and see where the bats actually come out. It's the most reliable way to find the real exits, and it often surprises the homeowner.
4. **Sealing the secondary gaps.** Every opening that isn't a main exit gets closed with metal flashing, hardware cloth or sealant that can stand up to Ontario winters. If you skip this, the colony simply moves to the next gap over.
5. **One-way doors on the main exits.** Tubes, cones or netting that let a bat crawl out but give it nothing to land on or grip coming back. Nobody handles the animals.
6. **Waiting.** The doors stay up for several warm nights, usually about a week. Rain and cold keep bats home, so a bad stretch of weather adds time.
7. **Final sealing.** Once we're sure the house is empty, the doors come off and those last openings get sealed the same way as the rest. If a chimney needs a cap or a gable vent needs a heavy-gauge screen, that happens now.
8. **The attic.** Then we deal with what the colony left behind, which is the next section.

Start to finish, most jobs take two or three weeks from inspection to final sealing. A big colony, a stretch of bad weather or a heavy cleanup can push that to four.

## The attic after they've gone

Bats leave a mess, and it's more than a smell problem.

Old guano can grow a fungus called *Histoplasma*. When the dried droppings get disturbed, the spores go into the air, and breathing them can cause a lung infection called histoplasmosis. For most healthy adults it's mild or nothing. For someone with a weak immune system, it can be serious. People most often get exposed when they start cleaning out a dusty attic or tearing into a ceiling without a mask. That's why we go in with respirators and suits, and why I'd ask you not to go up there with a broom.

Then there's the damage. Urine soaks into insulation and flattens it, so it stops doing its job. It stains drywall ceilings below the roost and corrodes metal flashing and nails over time. And bats can carry bat bugs and mites, which sometimes wander down into the house looking for a new host once the colony is out.

Our cleanup usually looks like this:

- Seal off the attic hatch and set up air movement so dust doesn't drift down into the house
- HEPA-vacuum the droppings and debris
- Bag and remove insulation that's soaked or full of guano
- Treat the wood with an enzyme product that breaks down the urine instead of covering the smell
- Blow in fresh insulation once everything's dry

On a cramped older attic with no floor and a low roof, this can take a while. We'll tell you up front how much we think needs to come out.

## Things I'd talk you out of

You can do some of this yourself. The dusk watch is free and useful. Keeping the attic hatch shut is smart. So is sealing around pot lights from the inside.

But I'd steer you away from a few things:

- **Foaming or caulking the hole you saw them use.** In summer you'll trap pups. Any time of year, you might trap adults, who then die in the wall or find a new way out, sometimes into the house.
- **Ultrasonic boxes, mothballs, bright lights.** None of them move a colony.
- **Getting up on a two-storey roof with an extension ladder** to chase a gap. Not worth the fall.
- **Picking up a bat on the ground,** even if it looks hurt. Gloves and a container, or call us.

And if a sealing job goes wrong with a protected species inside, the legal problem is yours.

## Should you put up a bat house?

It's not a bad idea. A bat house gives a displaced colony somewhere else to go, and bats are great to have around the yard. They eat a huge number of moths and beetles every night.

For it to work around here, mount it high, at least 12 to 20 feet up, facing south or southeast so it gets six or more hours of sun, and somewhere bats can fly straight in without branches in the way. A pole or the side of a garage works better than a tree.

But a bat house doesn't replace sealing the house. If the old entry is still open, they'll ignore the box and go home to your attic.

## Quick answers before you call

**Is it legal to get bats out of my house?**
Yes, through exclusion, at the right time of year. What you can't do is kill them, poison them or seal a maternity colony in during the summer.

**Can I stay in the house while it's being done?**
Yes. The doors are on the outside and the bats leave on their own at night. Just keep the attic hatch closed.

**Will they come back next year?**
Some will probably circle the house in spring and check the old spot, since bats remember a good roost. With every gap closed there's nothing for them to find, and they settle somewhere else.

**Can you do anything in the winter?**
Inspections, quotes and sealing the gaps nothing is using, yes. The exclusion itself waits until they're flying again.
