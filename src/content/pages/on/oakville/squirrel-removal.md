## Clues that point to a squirrel

Start with the clock. Squirrels work daylight hours, busiest early in the morning and again late in the afternoon. The typical call is somebody hearing quick scrabbling over the bedroom while they're making coffee and nothing at all once the house goes to bed. Slow, heavy movement at night is far more likely a raccoon. A faint, constant scratch inside a wall usually means mice.

Other things that point to squirrels:

- A rough, gnawed hole in a plastic vent, at a soffit corner or through the fascia board, often smaller than you'd think an animal could fit through
- Bright, fresh scrapes on aluminum fascia or the corner of an eavestrough
- Oak leaves and twigs poking out of the soffit, or packed into the gutter next to a vent
- Small, dark, oval droppings scattered near the attic hatch
- Insulation dragged into a heap and mixed with leaves and bark
- Thin, high squeaks from one spot in March, April, July or August, which usually means a litter
- A whiff of ammonia upstairs if it's been going on for a while

Where the noise is matters too. Squirrels like to nest close to their way in, so you'll often hear them along one outside wall or over a closet rather than dead centre in the ceiling.

## Greys, reds and the occasional flyer

Most Oakville attics have Eastern grey squirrels in them, and that includes the black ones you see everywhere. Same species, darker coat. Greys are the heavy chewers. They'll open one entry, keep widening it, and nest right beside it.

Red squirrels are smaller, chattier and fiercely territorial. I find them more on lots near the wooded ravines along Sixteen Mile Creek and Joshua's Creek. They like tight spaces, like wall cavities and long soffit runs, and they're harder to pin down because they'll keep several little holes going at once.

Then there are flying squirrels. They're out at night, they're small, and they get through gaps you'd never suspect, so people tend to blame mice or raccoons.

I was called to a home in Glen Abbey that backs onto the Sixteen Mile Creek valley, where the owners kept hearing soft thumps and skittering around 11 at night. They'd set mouse traps for weeks and caught nothing. Up in the attic, the droppings were tiny and there were a few separate nests, not one. When we came back at dusk and watched the roof, flying squirrels were gliding in and slipping under the end of the ridge vent where the cap didn't quite close. We put a one-way exit on that gap, closed the rest of the ridge with finer mesh than greys need, and sealed the end once they'd all gone.

## Where Oakville houses let them in

A squirrel has to gnaw constantly to keep its incisors worn down, so anything soft, loose or plastic on your roof gets tested sooner or later. Here's where I look first:

- **The soffit and fascia joint.** On a lot of the 1980s to 2000s two-storeys in Glen Abbey and River Oaks, this is thin aluminum or vinyl, and the seam slowly separates. A squirrel only needs a small start to work with.
- **Roof vents.** Plastic box vents and bathroom exhaust caps chew open quickly. Older metal vents often have screens that have rusted down to nothing.
- **Roof edges.** Loose shingles, rotted fascia boards, and valleys or dormers where two roof planes meet.
- **Utility lines.** Cable, phone and gas lines that run through the wall or soffit, often with a gap around them that nobody ever sealed.
- **Chimneys,** uncapped or with loose flashing.
- **Overhanging branches.** A big oak hanging over the gutter puts a squirrel on your roof without it ever touching the siding.

Townhouse rows in River Oaks and parts of College Park have their own wrinkle. The roof runs across several units, and a chewed vent on one is often the first of a few along the same line. If your neighbour's had squirrels, it's worth having yours looked at too.

A woman working from home in River Oaks called us because the scratching was right above her desk, every morning around the time her first video call started. From the yard, the roof looked fine. The trouble was lower: at some point a cable line had been run through the soffit, and the hole drilled for it was a lot bigger than the wire. A squirrel had chewed it out the rest of the way and was slipping in right alongside the cable. We put a one-way door on it, and once the squirrel was out we sealed around the line with a steel plate and collar, then went over the rest of the soffit while the ladder was up.

## Two nursery seasons a year

Grey squirrels around here usually raise two litters. The first round mates in January and February, with babies in March and April. The second brings babies in July and August, and the roof stays busy into September. For their first several weeks the young can't walk or climb, which means they can't use a one-way door.

Seal the hole while they're inside and the mother is shut out while her babies starve in the insulation. A locked-out mother doesn't give up, either. She'll go at the trim next to your patch until she's back in, and now you've got two holes instead of one.

So in those months, we check before anything goes on the roof. Depending on how old the babies are, we either hold off on the door for a couple of weeks until they can follow her out, or we gather them up from the nest in gloved hands and set them in a box with a heat pack right at her doorway. She'll ferry them to a tree nest herself, usually the same day.

Fall brings a different rush. The summer's young head off to find their own place, and the adults go looking for winter quarters, testing every vent and soffit seam on the street. Squirrels don't hibernate, so the ones that do get in spend the winter packing down the insulation and adding to the droppings.

## Traps and poison: why we skip them

Ontario's rules do let you deal with a squirrel that's damaging your house. But a squirrel caught in a cage has to go free within a day, and only within about a kilometre of where you trapped it. That's laid out on the province's page about [wild animals damaging private property](https://www.ontario.ca/page/harass-capture-or-kill-wild-animal-damaging-private-property). A squirrel covers that distance before lunch and comes straight back to the hole you haven't sealed. Catch a mother while she's nursing and the litter stays behind, upstairs, with nobody feeding it. The only time we use a live trap is when a squirrel ends up loose inside the house.

Poison is out, and so are glue traps. A poisoned squirrel crawls off somewhere tight to die, usually in a wall, and you live with the smell for weeks because nobody can reach it. It's also a risk to your pets and to the red-tailed hawks working over the Bronte Creek valley.

## What a nest does to an attic

Chewed wiring is the one that worries me most. Squirrels strip the plastic off electrical cable the same way they'd strip bark, and bare copper buried in insulation can start a fire long before anyone smells smoke. If we find any, have an electrician fix it before new insulation goes on top.

The insulation takes a beating too. They shred it for nesting and their weight packs the rest down, so it holds less heat, and you'll notice that on a long Oakville winter heating bill. Urine soaks in, stains the ceiling below and leaves an ammonia smell. Nests bring fleas, mites and ticks, and droppings carry bacteria. Leave it long enough and they'll start on the rafters and roof boards as well.

For a short stay in one corner, cleanup means taking out the nest and droppings, HEPA vacuuming and treating the area with disinfectant and deodorizer. After months of nesting, the soiled insulation gets bagged and removed and new insulation goes in, built back up to what Ontario attics are expected to have now, typically around R-50 or more. We seal off the hatch while we work so none of that dust drifts into the hallway.

Keep the photos and the report we give you. Some home insurance policies will cover part of the damage, especially wiring, and your insurer will want to see what was found.

## Keeping the roofline closed

After the sealing, your part is pretty simple. Have branches cut so they end 2 to 3 metres short of the roof. Clean the eavestroughs and make sure they sit snug against the fascia, because wet, rotting wood is exactly what squirrels go after. Move bird feeders out into the yard and pick up spilled seed. And once in spring and once in fall, walk around the house and look up at the roofline for anything chewed, lifted or hanging.
