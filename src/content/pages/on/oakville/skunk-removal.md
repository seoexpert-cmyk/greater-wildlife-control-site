## Skunk, or the raccoon from two doors down?

Before anybody digs anything, it helps to know who you're dealing with. Raccoons, groundhogs and skunks all like the space under a deck, and each one leaves its own calling card.

Things that point to a skunk:

- **Shallow little pits in the lawn,** each about the width of a toonie or a bit wider, poked in overnight while it hunts for grubs.
- **One tidy entrance hole** at the edge of a deck, shed, step or slab, worn smooth, with a small spill of fresh soil in front.
- **A faint musk right at the opening** even though nothing has sprayed. A den just smells like its tenant.
- **Five-toed prints** with long claws on the front feet, easy to spot in wet clay or a skiff of snow.
- **Comings and goings after sunset.** Skunks are night animals and are tucked away again before the sun is up.

If big sheets of sod have been peeled back and flipped over, I'd lean raccoon. A heap of dirt beside a wide hole, with an animal sunning itself at lunch, is a groundhog.

Want to check without getting close? Sprinkle a thin layer of flour on the patio stone in front of the hole before bed and look at it in the morning. Prints going in and out tell you it's active. A phone photo sent to us works too.

## Why skunks like Oakville so much

Oakville is built around creek valleys. Sixteen Mile Creek cuts right through the middle of town, Bronte Creek runs along the west side, and there are smaller ravines threading through Glen Abbey, Joshua Creek and Iroquois Ridge. A lot of homes back straight onto that green space, and skunks use the valley edges as their commute. Ravine-backed lots get visitors almost every summer.

Then there's the soil. Most of Oakville sits on heavy clay. It holds water, it heaves when it freezes, and it shrinks back when it dries out. Every few years that movement opens a fresh gap under a deck footing, a stoop or the edge of a garage slab. Skunks don't need much. A hole you could fit a coffee mug through is enough to get started, and their front claws will do the rest.

Where I find them most around town:

- Decks in Glen Abbey and River Oaks built low to the ground with open sides or tired lattice
- Older sheds in Old Oakville, College Park and Bronte that have sat on the same patio slabs for decades
- Poured concrete stoops where the clay under the slab has slumped away
- Additions and sunrooms on older backsplits in Falgarwood and Clearview, where the backfill has settled
- Newer builds up in Palermo and near Dundas, where the grading is still settling around the house
- Uncovered window wells, which are a trap more than a den

And the menu is good. Grubs in irrigated lawns, compost under a lid that doesn't latch, seed scattered on the ground, the cat's bowl on the back step, crabapples dropping off the tree. Shelter plus supper is all a skunk is looking for.

## Timing skunk removal in Oakville around the seasons

Skunks follow a pretty set yearly schedule here, and it decides what we can and can't do on your property.

- **Late February and March.** Breeding season. Males cover a lot of ground at night, so you'll see more of them on roads like Lakeshore and Rebecca, and smell more spray in the neighbourhood.
- **Late March and April.** Pregnant females pick out a den. Under your deck or shed is a top choice because it's dry and close to food. Sealing things up before this point is the cheapest fix there is.
- **May and June.** Kits are born, often four to seven of them. For the first several weeks they can't see and can't leave the den.
- **Late June and July.** The young start trailing their mother out at night, and by midsummer they're poking around on their own.
- **August to October.** The easiest window for exclusion work. The young are independent, everyone's above ground, and the clay is still soft enough to trench.
- **November through winter.** Skunks don't fully hibernate. They curl up in a winter den and doze through the cold, waking up to wander on mild nights.

The part that matters most is spring. Close off a hole in May and there's a decent chance a mother is outside and her kits are underneath. That leaves babies to die under your deck, a frantic mother digging new holes, and an odour problem a couple of weeks later that's much worse than the one you started with. It's also not something Ontario's wildlife rules let you do.

## What the job looks like, visit by visit

Here's the order things happen in when you book us.

**First visit: the walk-around.** I start at the hole you found, then keep going. Every side of the deck, the shed base, the stoop, the AC pad, window wells, the side yard, any retaining wall. Skunks nearly always keep a second exit, and missing it is the most common reason a job fails. Soil, prints, hair and smell tell me which openings are live.

**Working out who's home.** Is it a skunk at all? One animal or a mother with a litter? Fresh den or an old one? The time of year and what I see at the entrance answer most of it.

**A written quote.** You see what we plan to do and what it costs before anyone picks up a shovel.

**Shutting the spare exits.** Every extra opening gets closed until only one door is left.

**The one-way door.** We mount a hinged door snug over the main entrance. The skunk nudges it open from inside when it heads out to forage and can't push it back the other way. No bait, no cage, and nobody has to handle a skunk.

**A few nights of watching.** Usually two to five. I'll rake the soil smooth in front of the door so we can read which direction the tracks are pointing.

**The buried barrier.** Once the den's empty, we trench along the deck, shed or step, usually about 30 to 45 cm deep, and fasten heavy galvanized mesh to the structure. The bottom edge gets bent outward so it forms an L underground. A skunk digging at the edge runs into wire wherever it tries. We backfill, tamp it down and leave the grade tidy. Wooden skirting or chicken wire on its own won't stop a skunk, so we don't rely on either.

**Den cleanup.** We take out the worst of the soiled dirt and bedding and disinfect the spot, then go over it with an enzyme-based deodorizer.

A straightforward deck or shed is usually wrapped up in one to three weeks from the first call, often less. Kits in the den, frozen ground or a big perimeter push it longer.

### Mom, the kits and why we sometimes wait

I don't seal anything until I know whether a nursing mother is under there. At dusk I'll sit quietly by the opening and listen for thin, high squeaking, look for tiny prints next to the big ones, and line all of that up with the calendar.

If there's a litter, we have two ways to go. If the kits can be reached safely, we can lift them out, settle them in a warmed box right beside the entrance and put the door on, and the mother collects them and moves them herself. If they can't be reached, we wait until they're old enough to follow her out, then close it up once the whole family is gone. Baby season can add several weeks to a job. I'd rather tell you that up front than seal a litter in.

Other places worth a look with a flashlight:

- **Front stoops.** When the clay under a poured stoop slumps, it leaves a cave right next to the foundation, and the smell finds its way into the basement. We dig carefully around concrete so we don't make the hollow worse, then close the edges with mesh and flashing.

## The rules: the province, the Town and Halton health

Ontario's Fish and Wildlife Conservation Act gives property owners some room to act when wildlife is wrecking their place, but there are strings attached. If you live-catch a skunk, it has to go free within 24 hours, no farther than one kilometre from the spot you caught it. You can't drive it out past Milton. Poison and body-gripping traps aren't an option. The province's [rules for landowners](https://www.ontario.ca/page/harass-capture-or-kill-wild-animal-damaging-private-property) cover the rest.

That's the main reason we don't bother trapping skunks. You take the risk of a face full of spray, carry the cage a few streets over, and the skunk wanders home to the same open spot under your deck by the weekend.

On the Town side, a healthy skunk on private property is the owner's job to sort out. The Town's animal services deals with wildlife that's sick or injured. Oakville also has a by-law against feeding wildlife, and its [Wildlife and You page](https://www.oakville.ca/home-environment/pets-wildlife/wildlife-you/) covers that along with tips on bins and yards.

Health is the third piece. Skunks are one of Ontario's main rabies carriers, so a few situations go to the front of the line:

- **A skunk wandering in daylight** that's staggering, circling or strangely unafraid. Rabies and distemper both look like that. Bring kids and pets in and stay back.
- **Bites and scratches,** whether it's you, a child or the dog. Anyone who's been bitten needs a doctor and get in touch with Halton Region Public Health right away, because the shots given after an exposure do their job if they begin quickly. For a pet, phone your vet and check the rabies vaccine is current.

Leptospirosis, roundworm and the usual fleas and ticks can come along with a skunk too. Most dens never cause any of that, but it's one more reason not to share the yard with one.

## Your dog met the skunk. Now what?

First, keep the dog outside or in the garage. A sprayed dog loose in the house rubs that oil into the couch, the rugs and the furnace return.

If the eyes are red, rinse them gently with clean, lukewarm water. For the coat, plain shampoo won't cut it. Grab the brown bottle of 3% hydrogen peroxide from the medicine cabinet, add a spoonful or two of baking soda and a squeeze of dish soap, and mix it in an open bucket right before you use it. Work it into the fur, stay clear of the eyes, wait a few minutes and rinse it all out. The mix fizzes and gives off gas, so pour out the leftovers instead of capping them. A vet's skunk shampoo is a good backup.

If your dog's eyes still look sore by morning, or you think it may have been bitten, take it to the vet.

And if the dog got sprayed in your own yard, the skunk almost certainly lives there. It's going to happen again until the den is closed.

## Home remedies I'd skip

Most people try a couple of these before they call. Here's how they usually go:

- **Mothballs or an ammonia rag.** Skunks tolerate both, and mothballs are made for closets, not flower beds.
- **Shovelling the hole full.** In spring you could be burying kits alive. Any other time the skunk tunnels out a new entrance, often right up against your foundation.
- **Screwing a board over the opening.** Same problem as filling it, plus a skunk trapped inside will dig hard to get out.
- **A live trap from the hardware store.** More often than not you catch a cat. If you do catch the skunk, you're now carrying a scared skunk in a cage.
- **Predator urine and motion lights.** They might bother it for a few nights before it stops caring.
- **Poking under the deck with a rake.** A skunk that's about to spray usually stamps its front feet and raises its tail first. If you see that, back off slowly.

## Getting the smell out of the house

Sometimes the skunk is gone and the smell is still hanging around the basement. That's normal. Skunk musk is an oily blend of sulphur compounds that soaks into porous stuff like soil, insulation, carpet and drywall, and it tends to come back on humid days or after a rain.

For tonight, crack a few windows, run every exhaust fan you've got, and shut the furnace blower off for a bit so the ducts don't carry the smell to every room. A dish of baking soda or activated charcoal on the basement floor can soften it a little.

If it's still coming back after a week or so, something is still soaked. Usually the culprit is soil from the old den or insulation tucked against the sill plate nearby. We find the source and treat it with enzymes and oxidizers that break the oils apart rather than masking them. Anything that took a heavy dose, like a storage box or a section of batt insulation, is usually better tossed and replaced. Running a dehumidifier while it airs out helps a lot.

## Keeping the next skunk moving

With the den closed off, the goal is to give the next skunk no reason to slow down at your fence:

- Store bins inside the garage and roll them out on pickup morning, and keep compost in a bin with a clip-down lid
- Feed pets indoors, and sweep up under the bird feeder
- Pick up fallen apples, pears and crabapples
- If the lawn keeps getting pitted with little holes, sort out the grubs with your lawn care company
- Cover your window wells
- Move woodpiles and leftover lumber away from the back fence
- Walk the edges of your deck, shed and stoop each spring looking for new digging
- Building a deck, shed or play fort this year? Have the mesh dug in while it's going up. It costs far less than adding it later.
