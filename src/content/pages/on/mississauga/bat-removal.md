Some of the oddest bat calls I get come in the dead of winter. Somebody heads down to the laundry room in February, and there's a bat hanging off the wall next to the furnace, or flopping around on the basement floor looking half asleep. Their first question is always the same: how on earth did a bat get in here when it's minus ten outside?

The answer is it never left. Big brown bats will spend the whole winter tucked inside a wall or an attic, and on a mild day, or when the furnace warms up the wrong corner of the house, one wakes up confused and wanders toward the heat. That's the bat you see. The rest of them are usually still in the wall.

A lot of the bat removal in Mississauga we do starts with a moment like that, or a summer version of it: a rustle behind the drywall at dusk, a few dark pellets on the patio. So here's how I'd walk you through it on a first visit.

## How do you know it's more than one bat?

One bat in August can honestly be a youngster that took a wrong turn through an open screen door. A colony is different, and it leaves a trail.

Here's what tips me off:

- **Little piles of droppings that keep coming back.** They collect on a window ledge, the top of the AC unit, the patio, or on the attic insulation right under where the bats hang. Roll one between two popsicle sticks and it breaks into a dry, sparkly powder. That sparkle is bits of beetle and moth.
- **Dark, oily rub marks.** Bats use the same gap every night, and their fur leaves a brownish smear on the brick or the aluminum around it.
- **A rustling or a faint high squeak** in a wall or ceiling, mostly in the half hour after sunset and again before sunrise.
- **A sharp, sour smell** near the attic hatch on a hot day.

Two or more of those and it's a colony.

## Which bats are we talking about?

In Mississauga homes it's almost always one of two species.

The **big brown bat** is the usual tenant. It's hardy, it's fine with our winters, and it likes the warm pocket behind brick veneer. It isn't endangered, but it's protected under Ontario's [Fish and Wildlife Conservation Act](https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/97f41), so poisoning or trapping them is off the table.

The **little brown myotis** is smaller and much rarer now. White-nose syndrome tore through the caves where they hibernate, and the species is listed as endangered in Ontario. The females form maternity colonies in hot, dark attics in summer, and those roosts are protected too. If we find little browns, the timing gets even stricter, and occasionally the province needs to be involved before anyone touches the roost.

Telling them apart changes the rules, the timing and where we look. Big browns tend to work into wall voids. Little browns cluster in the open attic.

## Bat removal in Mississauga runs on the bats' calendar

People want the bats gone this week, and I get it. But the calendar decides.

- **Spring (roughly April to mid-May):** Bats come out of hibernation and the females start gathering. If they're flying every night, early exclusion can work.
- **Late May into August:** Maternity season. Pups are born in late May and June and can't fly for several weeks. Close the exits now and the mothers head out for the night's insects with no way back to their babies. A few weeks later you've got a dead nursery over your ceiling, and you've broken the law getting there.
- **Late August through fall:** This is the window. The young are flying, the whole colony goes out every night, and we can get everybody out in one go.
- **Winter:** A sleeping bat isn't going anywhere, one-way exit or not, and there's nothing for it to eat outside in January. This is when we inspect, seal the parts of the house nobody's roosting in, and plan for spring.

Getting this wrong isn't a slap on the wrist either. Fines under the provincial act can run into the tens of thousands, and they climb much higher when an endangered species is involved.

### Got bats in June? Here's the holding plan

We hold off on the exclusion. What we can do right away is cut the colony off from your rooms by weatherstripping the hatch and sealing wherever the ceiling has openings into the attic, like the chase the bathroom fan duct runs through. We can also seal secondary gaps on the outside that the bats clearly aren't using. That way nobody's waking up to a bat in the hallway while the colony finishes raising its pups.

## A bat loose in the house tonight

Before anything else, think about who was in that room. If someone was asleep in there, if a small child was in there, or if anyone touched it, don't let the bat go. Call Peel Public Health. Bat bites can be tiny enough to miss, and testing the bat can save someone a course of rabies shots. Only a small share of bats carry rabies, and the odds go up a lot with a bat that's grounded or acting sick, but it's not a gamble worth taking.

If nobody was exposed:

1. Shut the door to that room and keep the dog and the kids out.
2. Turn off the lights and open a window as wide as it goes.
3. Stay out for a while. A bat that can feel the outside air usually leaves on its own.
4. If it won't, wait for it to land, put on heavy work gloves, and trap it under a plastic tub with a sheet of cardboard slid beneath.

Then call us anyway. A bat in the living space, especially in winter, usually means a roost somewhere in the house.

## The gaps I find on Mississauga houses

A bat needs an opening of about a centimetre and a half, maybe as wide as a fat felt marker. Most of the ones I find you'd walk right past.

In Erindale, Streetsville and a lot of the older parts of Mississauga you've got 1970s and 80s split-levels, and every split-level has extra roof-to-wall joints where the lower roof butts into the upper storey. That step flashing loosens with age, and a bat only needs one lifted corner. Add aluminum soffit capped over the original wood and a few decades of freeze and thaw, and there are plenty of candidates.

The spots I check on every visit:

- Where the soffit meets the brick, especially at the corners
- Step flashing on split-levels and where an addition meets the original house
- Chimney flashing and the mortar around the chimney crown
- Gable vents and louvres with no screen, or a screen that's rusted out
- Weep holes and the J-channel on vinyl siding
- Exhaust vent flaps that don't close all the way

Location plays a part too. Homes near the lake, from Port Credit through Mineola and Lorne Park out toward Rattray Marsh in Clarkson, sit beside a lot of insect-rich air on summer nights. So do the streets along the Credit River valley and near Riverwood Conservancy. Bats feed over the water and the trees, then roost in whatever warm building is close by.

I had a call down in Port Credit, a couple of blocks from the lake, where the owners could hear soft scratching in the living room wall every evening and figured it was mice. There were no droppings anywhere inside. Outside, there was a little dark smear on the brick right where the chimney met the roof, and the flashing there had pulled away just enough to slip a finger under it. The bats were crawling in under that flashing and down into the wall cavity beside the chimney. Once the colony was out, the fix was new flashing, properly counter-flashed into the mortar. <!-- generic anecdote: confirm or replace with a real job -->

## Storefronts and flat roofs

Commercial buildings get bats too, just in different places. Along Lakeshore Road in Port Credit you've got older two- and three-storey storefronts with parapet walls, flat roofs, and flashing that's been patched more times than anyone remembers. Bats get in behind loose metal coping or around rooftop units and end up in the dead space above a drop ceiling.

Those jobs take more coordinating with owners, managers and tenants, and sometimes a lift or fall-arrest gear. The bat side is the same: same calendar, same one-way exits, same sealing.

## How bat removal in Mississauga actually works with us

1. **We ask a lot of questions on the phone.** What you've seen, where, when, and whether anyone has had contact with a bat.
2. **We inspect the whole outside.** Roofline, chimney, vents and wall joints, from ladders and the roof where it's safe. Then the attic. Where we can't see into a wall, a thermal camera shows where the colony is clustered.
3. **We watch them come out.** At sunset, one or two of us post up outside and count. That tells us which gaps are real exits.
4. **We seal every gap except the exits.** Metal flashing, galvanized mesh, mortar or sealant, depending on the spot.
5. **We fit one-way devices on the exits.** Tubes or netting that a bat can crawl out through but can't find its way back into.
6. **We wait.** Usually a week or two, longer if a cold or rainy stretch keeps the bats in.
7. **We take the devices off and seal those last openings.** Any repairs, like a new chimney cap, a gable screen or a soffit section, happen here.

Step three is where a lot of the real work gets done. On a Clarkson house with a wooden louvre up in the attic gable, the owners were sure the bats were coming out of the soffit above their back door, because that's where the droppings landed. At dusk, every single bat dropped out from between the louvre slats two metres higher up. There was no screen behind the louvre at all, so the colony had the whole attic. We put the exclusion netting over the louvre, sealed everything else, and once they were out we added heavy mesh behind the slats. <!-- generic anecdote: confirm or replace with a real job -->

We stand behind the sealing we do, in writing, and the paperwork spells out which areas are covered.

## The attic after they've moved out

A colony that's been there a few summers leaves a crust of guano on the insulation and urine stains on the wood. Dry guano can grow *Histoplasma*, the fungus behind histoplasmosis, a lung infection you get from breathing the spores when the droppings are stirred up. Most healthy adults shrug it off, but not everyone does. That's why I go up there in a respirator and coveralls, and why I'll ask you not to go at it with a broom.

On our end, the hatch gets taped off first so none of the dust drifts downstairs. The droppings come out with a HEPA vacuum, and any insulation that's matted or stained gets bagged and hauled out. We spray the joists and sheathing with an antimicrobial deodorizer, let it dry, and blow in new insulation. Done right, the sour smell on the top floor goes with it.

## What doesn't work (and one thing that makes it worse)

- **Ultrasonic plug-ins and mothballs.** I've walked into plenty of attics with both and a full colony.
- **Climbing a two-storey roof yourself.** Not worth a broken wrist over a gap.
- **Caulking the hole you saw them use.** This is the one that makes it worse. A trapped colony doesn't give up. Bats start crawling around inside the walls looking for daylight, and the nearest way out may be a gap around your pot lights. The ones that don't find one die in there, and in summer that includes every pup.

What you can do: a dusk watch, keep the hatch closed, and seal around pot lights from inside.

## A few things people ask me at the door

**Will a bat house in the yard keep them out of my attic?**
Not by itself. A warm attic beats a wooden box every time. Where it helps is after we close the house, because the evicted colony has somewhere nearby to go instead of testing your neighbour's soffit.

**Does all the insulation have to go?**
Not always. A light scatter of droppings near one gable can be vacuumed out. It's the stained, matted, smelly sections under a long-term roost that need replacing, and we'll show you which is which before we price it.
