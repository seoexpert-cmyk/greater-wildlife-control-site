## Who's making the mess?

**Pigeons.** Feral rock pigeons, here all year and nesting several times a season on ledges, balconies, garages and rooftop units. Their droppings are our biggest cleanup headache.

**European starlings.** Glossy, speckled, noisy and very good at squeezing into things. Sign cabinets, gable vents, fascia ends, exhaust hoods. They come back to the same cavity year after year if you let them.

**House sparrows.** Small and brown, and the bird you'll find in most residential vents. They'll fit through a gap you'd swear was too small, roughly a centimetre and a bit, and raise more than one brood in the same spot.

**Gulls.** Ring-billed and herring gulls. This close to the lake, they nest on flat plaza and restaurant roofs and patrol anywhere there's food.

**Canada geese.** Office-park ponds, stormwater ponds and sports fields. Territorial in spring and messy all year.

## What the law lets you touch, and when

Most native birds are covered by the federal Migratory Birds Convention Act. Under the [Migratory Birds Regulations, 2022](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/avoiding-harm-migratory-birds/fact-sheet-nest-protection-under-mbr-2022.html), you can't disturb or destroy a nest that has live birds or viable eggs in it. For a short list of species, even an empty nest stays protected for a set waiting period before it can come down. Gulls and geese are covered, and a gull nest on a roof can turn a simple job into a permit question. Ontario adds its own rules through the Fish and Wildlife Conservation Act, and the province has a plain page on [removing bird nests or eggs](https://www.ontario.ca/page/remove-bird-nests-or-eggs).

Pigeons, starlings and house sparrows are introduced species and aren't covered by the federal act, which gives us more room. Even so, I don't pull out a nest full of chicks just because I legally can. We time the work so we're not leaving babies to die in a sign or a wall.

Most birds here nest from about March through August, which is why the off-season is the time to bird-proof.

If we ever live-capture a bird or any other animal, it stays local. Ontario's rule is that wildlife gets released within about a kilometre of where it was caught, within 24 hours.

## Birds in the vents at home

On houses, the usual suspects in vents are sparrows and starlings. Dryer vents, bathroom fan vents, kitchen exhaust hoods. That little louvred flap is supposed to keep things out, but a bird that's watched it swing open in the wind knows it's a door.

It isn't only noise. Nesting material mixed with lint in a dryer duct is a fire risk. A bathroom fan that can't push air out leaves moisture behind, and that's how you end up with mould on the ceiling.

How you'd know: the flap stays propped open on a calm day, there's a little pile of twigs and feathers on the siding or the ground under it, towels are coming out of the dryer damp, or the bathroom mirror stays fogged long after a shower. Sometimes you'll just hear scratching and peeping right behind the fan grille.

Our fix is to find out what bird it is and whether there are young, pull the nest and clear the duct all the way to the appliance, sanitize it, and put a heavy-gauge metal guard over the outside opening. The flimsy plastic covers from the big-box store go brittle after a winter or two, and I pull a lot of them off with a nest right behind them.

## Droppings eat buildings, slowly

Pigeon droppings are acidic, and they don't rinse off with the rain. Give them a few seasons and you'll see the finish dulled on sign faces, rust starting on metal flashing and railings, and white stains soaked into the precast. On flat roofs, nest debris and droppings wash into the drains. Come January, the water that can't get away freezes, and that's when the leak calls start.

Built-up droppings can grow *Histoplasma*, the fungus behind histoplasmosis, and can carry salmonella and E. coli. Pigeons can also carry psittacosis, which shows up as a fever and cough. The risk isn't the odd splat on a car. It's the old, dry layer on a ledge or in an attic, because the moment somebody dry-sweeps it, the dust is in their lungs. For a business, add a slippery sidewalk and a storefront that looks neglected.

Avian flu has also been [confirmed in Mississauga](https://www.mississauga.ca/services-and-programs/animals-and-pets/wildlife-in-mississauga/wild-birds/). The risk to people is low, but it's one more reason not to handle sick or dead birds bare-handed.

When we clean up, we wear respirators, coveralls, gloves and eye protection. We wet the droppings down or HEPA-vacuum them instead of sweeping, double-bag the waste, and finish with a disinfectant. If droppings have soaked into attic insulation, that insulation comes out. If they've gotten into ductwork, the ducts get cleaned too.

## Hardware that holds up, and what I'd skip

**Netting** is the most reliable fix for loading bays, courtyards, balconies and eaves. It has to be UV-stable, tight and fastened with hardware that won't rust.

**Spikes** stop pigeons landing on ledges, sign tops and parapets. The tips are blunt. A pigeon simply can't get its feet down between them, so it moves on.

**Electric track** gives a mild, harmless jolt when a bird lands, and suits storefront ledges where spikes would look out of place.

**Vent guards and mesh** close the cavities. Gaps as small as a centimetre or so need attention because sparrows will use them.

What I'd skip as a standalone fix: plastic owls and falcons, reflective tape, scare balloons and sound boxes. Birds get used to them fast. Optical gel and visual deterrents can help alongside real barriers, but on their own they don't hold.

## Do it before March

Bird-proofing in November through February is easier and cheaper than chasing an active nest in May. No eggs to wait on, no permit questions, and the birds show up in spring to find their spot already closed. That goes double for commercial roofs where gulls nest.

In the meantime, keep dumpster lids shut, take feeders down near the building, clear clutter out of balcony and dock corners, and snap dated photos of any damage for insurance.

## Things plaza managers and homeowners ask me

**We already had spikes put up and the pigeons are still here. Why?**
Usually the spikes only cover part of the ledge, or the birds are nesting somewhere spikes can't help, like behind a sign or on top of a unit. Pigeons will hang around an old spot for weeks, but if the whole area is closed off and the droppings are cleaned up, they move on.

**Can I clean up a little bit of droppings myself?**
A small patch of fresh droppings, sure, with gloves, a mask, and wetting it down first. Large dry buildups, anything indoors, or anything in an attic, leave to us.
