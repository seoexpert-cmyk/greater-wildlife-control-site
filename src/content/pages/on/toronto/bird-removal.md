If your bathroom fan has started chirping, it isn't the motor. Some bird has found the vent hood on the outside wall, pushed past the flap, and is building a nest in the duct. Same goes for a dryer that's suddenly taking two cycles to dry a load, or a scratchy, fluttery sound in the soffit every morning in May.

Most of the bird removal in Toronto we do is exactly that kind of thing: starlings and house sparrows in vents and soffits on houses, and pigeons on balconies, ledges and rooftop units on condos and commercial buildings. Now and then it's gulls on a flat roof near the lake. The work isn't complicated, but there are a few rules you need to know about, because a lot of birds and their nests are protected by law and the season matters.

Below is what I'd tell you on a site visit: which birds cause the trouble, what you're allowed to do about them, how we get them out, and how we keep them from coming straight back.

## Which birds are we usually dealing with?

**Pigeons.** These are feral rock pigeons, and they're the main problem on buildings downtown and in midtown. They nest on balconies, window ledges, parking garages, under signs and behind rooftop equipment. They'll raise several broods a year in a sheltered spot, and once they pick a place they come back to it with a stubbornness that's honestly impressive.

**Starlings and house sparrows.** Both are introduced species, both are small and bold, and both love a hole in the side of a house. Dryer vents, bathroom and kitchen exhaust vents, gaps in soffits, the hollow letters on a storefront sign. If you've got birds in a vent, it's almost always one of these two.

**Gulls.** Ring-billed and herring gulls hang around the waterfront and set up on flat commercial roofs, malls and industrial buildings. During nesting season they get loud and aggressive, and they'll dive at anyone who walks too close.

**Canada geese.** Mostly a problem in parks, on condo courtyards and around corporate lawns with a pond. They're aggressive in spring and leave a startling amount of droppings.

Why does the species matter so much? Because the law treats them very differently, and because each one goes after a different part of the building.

## Is it legal to remove a bird's nest in Toronto?

Short answer: it depends on the bird and whether the nest is active.

Most native birds, including gulls and Canada geese, are covered by the federal Migratory Birds Convention Act. Their nests are protected while there are eggs or live young in them, and you need a permit from the Canadian Wildlife Service to interfere with an active nest. The usual one for property problems is a [Damage or Danger permit](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/migratory-bird-permits/damage-danger/instruction-sheet.html), and you generally have to show that you've already tried non-lethal steps to keep the birds away. Plan on a couple of weeks for one to come through.

Ontario's Fish and Wildlife Conservation Act adds provincial rules on top of that for wild birds, their nests and their eggs.

Pigeons, starlings and house sparrows are the exceptions. They're introduced species, so the federal migratory bird law doesn't cover them, and the provincial rules treat starlings and house sparrows differently from native songbirds too. That's why an empty pigeon nest on your own balcony can generally just be cleared away. Even so, when there are eggs or chicks in a nest, we slow down and look at the timing before we touch anything.

Here's the rule of thumb I give people:

- **Can't say for sure what the bird is? Leave the nest alone** and send us a photo.
- **Eggs or babies in it? Leave it alone** until we've had a look.
- **Native bird nesting season** runs roughly April through July. Work done before or after that window is a lot simpler.

The City doesn't remove birds from private property. That's on the owner. Toronto does have a [page on pigeons](https://www.toronto.ca/community-people/animals-pets/pets-in-the-city/pigeons/) that covers the bylaws on feeding birds and keeping domestic pigeons, which is worth a read if a neighbour is the one putting out seed.

## Birds in vents: the call we get most

Vents are a perfect nest site from a bird's point of view. They're a dry, sheltered tube, they're warm, and most vent hoods have a flap a starling can push open with its head. Once one gets in, it hauls in grass, twigs, plastic and feathers until the duct is stuffed.

That's more than an annoyance. A dryer vent packed with nesting material on top of lint is a fire risk. A blocked bathroom fan can't move moisture out of the room, so you start getting mildew on the ceiling. And when the chicks hatch, the noise is right there in the wall.

I remember a bathroom fan in a split-level out in Etobicoke where the owner said it sounded like the fan was full of crickets. From outside you could see the vent hood flap was jammed half open with grass. When I pulled the hood, the nest went back about an arm's length into the duct, with a couple of starling chicks calling for food. We waited until they'd left the nest, pulled the whole thing out with a vent brush and a lot of patience, then put a proper guard over the outlet. <!-- generic anecdote: confirm or replace with a real job -->

For vents, the fix is usually:

1. Confirm what bird it is and whether there are eggs or chicks.
2. Wait for young to leave if they're close to flying, or get them out the right way if the nest can come out.
3. Clear the whole duct, right back to the fan or the dryer, instead of only the end you can see.
4. Clean and disinfect, because nest material is full of mites and droppings.
5. Install a sturdy metal vent guard that lets air out but keeps birds from getting in.

The cheap plastic covers from the hardware store tend to crack in the cold or clog with lint. I'd skip them.

## Pigeons on the balcony

If you live in a condo downtown, around Liberty Village or near the lake, you probably already know the pigeon routine. One shows up, then two, then you find twigs behind the planter, and a week later there's a nest in the corner behind the patio chairs and droppings on everything.

We had a call from a high floor in a Liberty Village tower where the owner had stored a folded patio table against the glass for the winter. By spring, a pair of pigeons had built a nest in the triangle behind it and laid eggs. The owner had tried a plastic owl and some reflective tape, and the pigeons were standing next to the owl. We cleared the nest once it was empty, cleaned and disinfected the whole balcony, and installed netting across the opening so the birds couldn't land at all. <!-- generic anecdote: confirm or replace with a real job -->

What usually works on a balcony:

- **Netting** across the open side. It's the only thing that keeps pigeons off completely. Good netting is fine black mesh that's hard to see from the street.
- **Spikes or bird wire** on railings and ledges where netting isn't allowed.
- **Getting rid of clutter.** Anything that makes a sheltered corner, like a stored table, boxes or a big planter, is an invitation.

One thing to know: many condo boards have rules about what you can attach to a balcony. We're used to that. We'll work with your property manager or board to get the installation approved and done in a way that fits the building.

## Why bird droppings are more than a cleaning job

Bird droppings aren't just ugly. Built-up droppings can carry the fungi behind histoplasmosis and cryptococcosis, and the bacteria behind psittacosis and salmonella. Fresh droppings outside aren't much of a risk. The trouble starts when a big pile dries out and gets disturbed, say when you sweep it or hit it with a hose, and the dust goes up into the air where you're breathing.

That's why I wear a respirator for big cleanups, and why I'll tell you not to dry-sweep a heavily soiled balcony, attic or loading dock.

The buildings suffer too:

- **Masonry and metal.** Droppings are acidic. Over time they etch stone, brick, paint and metal. On heritage buildings with limestone trim, that damage is permanent.
- **Drains and eavestroughs.** Nests and droppings clog roof drains and downspouts. Water backs up, and in a Toronto winter that turns into ice damming and leaks.
- **Rooftop equipment.** Gulls and pigeons nest around HVAC units. Debris and droppings get into fans and intakes and corrode the parts.
- **Solar panels.** Droppings cut output, and birds nesting underneath can chew on wiring.
- **Walkways and entrances.** Wet droppings are slippery, which is a real liability on a commercial property or a busy condo entrance.

Nesting material also carries mites, fleas and lice, and those don't stay put once the birds leave.

## How we get birds out and keep them out

The main idea is simple. Chasing birds off doesn't last. Taking away the spot they want does. Here's how we approach it.

### Look first

Every job starts with an inspection. We figure out the species, check for active nests, find every place the birds are getting in or landing, and look at how bad the mess is. On a house that's a ladder and a walk around. On a commercial roof or a high balcony it can mean roof access, binoculars and sometimes a camera on a pole. We take photos and measurements so the quote matches the actual job.

### Let them leave

If birds are inside a soffit, attic or enclosed roof space, we can install a one-way exclusion device over the opening. The birds can get out but can't get back in, and nobody has to handle them. We only do this after confirming there are no eggs or young inside that would be trapped.

### Close it up

This is the part that actually solves the problem:

- Metal vent guards and screens on every vent
- Soffit repairs and metal flashing over gaps
- Stainless or galvanized mesh behind signs, under solar panels and around rooftop units
- UV-resistant netting over balconies, loading docks, open sides of parking garages and equipment bays

Everything has to stand up to freeze and thaw, heavy snow and summer sun, so we use metal and commercial-grade netting, not plastic.

### Make the landing spots useless

For ledges, signs, parapets and rooflines:

- **Spikes.** Stainless or plastic, they don't hurt the birds, they just leave nowhere to stand.
- **Tensioned bird wire.** Low-profile, good on heritage buildings where you don't want visible hardware.
- **Electric track.** A thin strip that gives a mild static pulse. Birds hate it, it doesn't hurt them, and it's nearly invisible from the street.
- **Optical gel.** Little dishes of gel that birds seem to read as fire. Useful where nothing else can be mounted.

### What I'd skip

Plastic owls, scare balloons, reflective tape and sound boxes. Honestly, city pigeons figure them out in a week or two. They can help as part of a bigger plan, but on their own they're money down the drain.

## Gulls on flat roofs and geese on lawns

Big flat roofs near the lake, from Etobicoke's waterfront to the Port Lands, get gulls every spring. They nest right on the gravel or the membrane, and once eggs are down you're into permit territory. The answer is planning ahead: netting or wire grids over the parts of the roof they like, and starting in late winter before they settle in.

Geese are a different job altogether. They want open grass next to water with a clear view of anything coming. Changing that layout with taller plantings along the pond edge or low fencing between the water and the lawn does more than any amount of chasing. Geese are federally protected, so any nest work needs a permit. If you manage a property with a goose problem, call us early in the year and we'll go over what can be done.

Here's the usual order of things:

1. **You call or send a message.** We'll ask what bird you think it is, where it is, and whether it's inside the house. Photos help a lot.
2. **Inspection.** We look at the whole area, inside if birds are getting into an attic or unit, and figure out the species, the nest status and every way in.

4. **Removal and exclusion.** One-way devices where needed, then the guards, netting, spikes and repairs. Small fixes, like a broken soffit or loose flashing, usually happen on the same visit.

## Can you handle it yourself?

Some of it, sure. Here's my honest take.

**Fine to do yourself:**

- Clear an empty pigeon nest from your own balcony, wearing gloves and a mask
- Wash off small amounts of fresh droppings outside with soap and water
- Move the stored stuff and clutter that's giving pigeons a corner to nest in
- Stop feeding birds near the house, and ask neighbours to do the same

**Leave it to us:**

- Any nest with eggs or chicks where you aren't sure of the species
- Anything above a first-storey ladder, especially on a condo or a steep roof
- Big piles of dried droppings, particularly indoors or in an attic
- Netting and hardware that has to survive a Toronto winter

The big DIY mistakes I see are breaking the law without meaning to by pulling a protected nest, and blocking off a vent or soffit with a bird still inside.

## Will the birds come back?

They'll try. Pigeons especially have strong homing instincts and keep checking a spot they used to use. If the place they want is sealed or netted and the smell is gone, they give up and move on.

The cases where birds come back almost always trace to one of three things: a gap that got missed, a deterrent that was the wrong type for the bird, or a cleanup that left droppings and nest material behind. That's why we do all three parts on the same job.

## Short answers to the usual bird questions

**Is it legal to remove a pigeon nest from my balcony?**
An empty pigeon nest on your own property, generally yes. If you're not sure it's a pigeon, or there are eggs or chicks, send us a photo first.

**Can I clean up bird droppings myself?**
Small amounts outside, yes, with gloves, a mask and soap and water. Don't dry-sweep big deposits, and leave attics and enclosed spaces to us.

**Are my pets at risk?**
Dogs and cats can pick up mites from old nests and get into droppings. Geese can also go after dogs in spring. Clearing and cleaning the area takes care of most of it.

**Can you just remove the birds and skip the hardware?**
We can, but I'd be honest with you that they'll be back. An empty vent or an open balcony is the same invitation next week that it was last week. The guard, the netting or the spikes are what make the removal stick, and they usually cost less than coming back to do the whole thing twice.

**What if a bird is actually inside the house?**
Close the door to that room, open a window, turn off the lights inside and leave it alone for a while. Most birds will find the bright opening. If it's trapped in a wall, a chimney or the attic, call us instead of opening things up yourself.

Yes, we cover the GTA, including North York, Scarborough and Etobicoke, and the surrounding cities.
